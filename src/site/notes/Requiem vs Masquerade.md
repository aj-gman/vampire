#opinion #article

My experience with vampire fiction was limited before being presented with VTM by a friend. I started my GM experience with Cyberpunk 2020, which was a rough find since I could barely understand the rulesets and combat made no sense because it was so difficult to wrap my head around it. It did not help that I had no hangout spot to run the sessions from, so I'd be with my friends on the street one day, another in a food court and some days in or around libraries. Nevertheless, I was feeling fatigue from cybernetic enhancements right after Cyberpunk RED had rolled around (of which I had mixed feelings). So, when my friend ran me a session of V5, I was amazed at the potential it had. Oh boy, did it change me. Almost overnight, everything I dreaded about Cyberpunk's system was thrown out the window and dramatically changed. Humanity stopped being a point tracker for how much I can push it and instead became a philosophical struggle against an inner demon. And the clans? Oh boy, the Roles of Cyberpunk had woo-ed me in the past, but I had slipped into VTM-mania hard and I saw the Cyberpunk roles just melt into the Clans, shaping them however I wanted to.

I was never satisfied with any game system. I genuinely believe there's a system out there for everybody. **However, there is no perfect game system to satiate the thirst we have for storytelling, and we can never have that.** There are different systems for different itches that we need scratching: _personal horror, action-adventure, spy-thriller, psychological debate, gods among men, powergaming._ Sometimes, the system needs to be in tune with the themes of the story: [Night's Black Agents](https://pelgranepress.com/nights-black-agents/) pits you as unknowing hunters uncovering a massive conspiracy of vampires in your local town. This has the proportions of going global, sure, but the key factor that differentiates this game from [White Wolf's World of Darkness](https://whitewolf.fandom.com/wiki/World_of_Darkness) isn't the mechanical part, but rather, the imperfect information that the players (and the GM) have access to and must work with. Sure, you could set up a [Night's Black Agents](https://pelgranepress.com/nights-black-agents/) campaign in the [WoD](https://whitewolf.fandom.com/wiki/World_of_Darkness), but that wouldn't have the same impact[^1] because, knowingly or not, the players and the GM mold the game into a broken mirror of Vampire, recognizing those specific tropes, those specific clans and abilities.

I would argue that this **imperfect information** stands at the core of any worthwhile play experience because it represents the lens through which the table pursues the themes and storylines of any universe. Imperfect information guarantees a certain kind of immersion, which is often overlooked when considering what system to run a campaign in.

> _Always try new things. Systems, homebrew, classes and anything that spices up play. That way, you'll find out your mainstays and your hard no's. For example, I have discovered I do not enjoy playing Dungeons & Dragons because I, for one reason or another, simply do not enjoy the realm of medieval fantasy. However, I've still managed to play [Mörk Borg](https://morkborg.com) because of the way the system and the setting make me experience that specific theme._

### About Masquerade.

There is something nonchalantly cool about the clans presented in Masquerade, especially for a player/GM coming into first contact through V5 and having to find lore bits from other books incidentally. At the same time, this quickly became one of my main complains the more I read about Requiem: in VTM, vampires can be everything, but they are sorted in clans in a way that perpetuates and creates stereotypes, without giving way to interesting relations unless you go outside the canon and outside the norm. I hate the fact that vampires are both necromancers and blood wizards, even if these on their own sound like very cool concepts. The 13 clans don't feel like actual tribes, rather, they are role-oriented and very hierarchy-prone, to the point that you could call them human stereotypes, something that Requiem threw out the window.

The way I see it, Masquerade is a game about rock solid foundations, which is both its strength and its downfall. In order to interpret anything in the VTM universe as a GM, you have to have a basic understanding of the lore of each clan and each sect. At the same time, the players are constrained in crystalized structures that rarely deviate from their declared purposes. The Camarilla is most often headed by a Ventrue or a Toreador, and the Sabbat is usually ruled by a Tzimitzi or, maybe, a Lasombra. The Anarchs are done so poorly its not even worth talking about them: never have I been able to use them as more than just a disjointed distraction that the Camarilla manipulates here and there. If the Cammies are a rules-of-law hierarchy, the Sabbat are a theological rule.

**This dynamic limits the interplay between the organizations**, especially as the earlier you go in rulebook history, the more the Sabbat are just terrorists that want to kill everybody. However, one could argue that this dynamic is perhaps better for a newer table, because it is predictable and reliable: the Cammie Ventrue will always be annoying, the Sabbat Tzimitzi will always be a funny little Ventrue with an attitude about his home and an accent[^2]. Overall, I found it stale after playing around with it for half a year of sessions: there is difficulty in humanizing the Sabbat but it is futile, because everything that you might change at a local level is rendered obsolete by the overarching globalization of vampire culture - huh, never thought I'd say that, but here we are. Masquerade feels too centralized for me, because at some point it becomes clear that there are some individuals in the universe that control everything to a nauseating level - whether it is the Camarilla's higher-ups, the Inconnu or anyone else that oversees the entire globe. To me, that feels unrealistic, because if the Camarilla was so smart, it would have eradicated the Anarch "rabble" by now.

However, I really like the Discipline distribution in VTM:V20. I like that Dominate is not a bloated Discipline, reserved for only a few clans (The fact that the Tzimitzi don't have Dominate and instead have Auspex is so much more intuitive!). That is as far as my V20 knowledge stretches. This is especially relevant since **I absolutely despise the way Disciplines in V5 have become so pathetic and narrow, limiting the character in doing the same two actions repeatedly**.

Take for example Obfuscate, which is a foundational Discipline in all games, from V5[^3] to VTR[^4].

In V5, Obfuscate's ability to go invisible is split into two, depending on the sense: vision or sound. This might be because it needs to account for the removal of multiple signature Disciplines like Quietus. Next up, taking additional points in the Discipline allows you to gain access to other abilities or upgrade the preexisting ones into more powerful versions. Instead of having a massive blanket area of use but requiring niche precision in order to pull off in a subtle and beautiful manner, V5 gives you three or more niche tools you can use (almost like DnD spells) and asks you to specialize your character. So, either you're a specialist that becomes useless once they encounter something they cannot reasonably circumvent[^5] or you're a jack of all trades who isn't very good at anything particularly. That doesn't make vampires scary or interesting.

In VTR, you have only one ability every level, but they are much more vague. You become a forgettable stranger, you take on the form of a stereotype, you hide objects and disappear into nothingness. It makes the ability much more lethal and much more like a sandbox you can play around with.

### About Requiem.

I discovered Requiem through a Reddit post while [designing Bucharest for my VTM campaign](https://gmantabletops.miraheze.org/wiki/Joytown_by_Night "Joytown by Night"), recommending the [Damnation City](https://whitewolf.fandom.com/wiki/Damnation_City) sourcebook as the best for crafting a gritty urban setting. I picked it up and, even if I didn't quite understand the covenants, I took some relevant ideas. I really appreciated the way the book constantly reminded you that vampires have been around for a while, which means they've developed secret languages to communicate, signs that are everywhere but never evident to the naked eye. Graffiti became a meaningful tool to communicate, neighborhoods became hotbeds of storytelling depending on the mood of the people living in them and whatever blight was affecting them at the time.

And then, the punk element was no more. For a GM coming from a pure "-punk" background, it was rough adjusting to the idea that maybe it's not always about fighting some righteous fight in the streets against an unbeatable opponent. It wasn't even about not being edgy - let's face it, Requiem is far edgier than V5. Rather, I finally was given the tools to craft a story where the players were in control of their actions, but also their own story: their relationships for once, mattered, because every mechanic somehow related back to the narrative. This was amazing. I had a real session zero for the first time in 4 years of GM-ing where we didn't just draft characters and looked at 5000+ Merits to choose from. We used the [Climbing The Ladder](https://gmantabletops.miraheze.org/wiki/Climbing_The_Ladder "Climbing The Ladder") system to generate bonds with non-playable characters and the world and the players had so much freedom because they could talk to each other, get ideas and eventually have their NPCs interact with one another. Cyberpunk 2020 had you roll NPCs related to the Player Characters, but their motivations were usually tied to a singular player. Now, there was a complex web of relations that we had to keep up and remember - and when we didn't, the story got even more interesting, because things would take unexpected turns!

Requiem stressed out from the beginning the fact that it was local, that it was decentralized and that it was pick and choose, something I admired from the start[^6]. The covenants, although initially quite difficult to wrap my head around, gave way to this idea perfectly: each city has its local leader, yes, a Prince, but cultural localization means so much now: Crones in Scandinavia practice a certain kind of magic, worship a different kind of deity than the Crones in the Carpathians. Also, the five-five-five system was, to me, refreshing, because it allowed interplay at a higher level than before[^7], with covenants taking center stage through the NPCs that represented them: you could get a feeling of who did what just based on how others reacted. I really like the VTR covenants, because they have personality and grit and because they are not just split between Good, Evil & Chaotic. Furthermore, there is no distinct association you can draw between Masquerade and Requiem.

- The Invictus is not just a rebrand of the Camarilla. Yes, they have rules and act like the Ivory Tower, but they are also successful businessmen and they promote youngbloods just as much as Elders if they truly believe in them. Most of all, they are the only ones actually keeping any semblance of order against the tidal wave that the other covenants represent.
- The Carthians are not just anarchist punks. They are often responsible for the cruelest systems put in place by vampires, because they are political activists that often only see their goal and easily justify their means.
- The Lancea et Sanctum are not just lore-keepers or profound Christians. They are the servants of a higher power, they are respectful and they often try to keep themselves from committing atrocities.
- The Crones are not just shovelheads without order. Their protective Mother allows them to be a cult of powerful women, first and foremost, which gives an interesting curveball to the patriarchy commonly presented in VTM. But the Crones aren't just a matriarchal society: they command shadowy magic that is deeply personal, which makes them a potent ally.
- The Ordo Dracul, a rebranding of the Tzimitzi clan into something truly scary. No more blood sorcery or pseudo-surgery, we now have undead researchers sitting in their empty castles ruminating over centuries-old math equations in order to achieve absolute perfection. They are eldritch horrors, sure, but they are rigorously scientific in their nightmarish logic. Also, they are not all Ventrue-like, which makes it all-the-more terrifying.

### Conclusions

I'll be the first to admit that the World/Chronicles of Darkness system is not perfect. One of the main flaws that's omnipresent is the way the Merits system is designed as a compendium of points and dots to spend whenever you need. Sometimes, merits feel so empty and barren and reading splatbooks feels like they only released them in order to ram fifteen pages of kind-of-cool merits at the end of the book. It's harrowing to have to sit through character creation spending points to gain merits, especially since the list is either way too long and laborious[^8] or too short to meaningfully describe your character[^9].

However, in my honest opinion, if you want to play pretend with a bunch of your friends/enemies/lovers/strangers, acting as if you were all blood-sucking creatures of the night, you are much better off trying Requiem because it can scratch the itch that Masquerade V5 won't be able to. VTM's community has been struggling with the idea that player characters often feel as if they were just ["superheroes with fangs"](https://www.reddit.com/r/vtm/search/?q=superheroes+with+fangs&cId=b1d84823-71b8-44d6-b17d-268d83fb0dc9&iId=cf0aadf5-37cd-426a-aac5-6ac85899fee8), especially when playing VTM as a Cammie/Anarch mixture of trying to do good while staying away from the law. Requiem gives you actual beasts to contend with, it gives you 5 archetypes of vampire (not 13 Clans), each with their own quirks and desires. It gives you different voices for your Beast. It encourages players to proactively involve themselves in each other's stories[^10] instead of waiting idly by while somebody interacts with their Touchstone.

Gritty, dark stories about losing yourself to an addiction you can barely understand are much easier told in Requiem because the mechanics actively encourage this type of storytelling. Requiem constantly reminds you to have a clear theme to your Chronicle/Arc/Session and gives you tips to support that. Everything in Requiem points to a greater narrative, usually one neither you nor the players actively understand. Requiem is a game about monsters struggling to pretend to be human and actively desiring it, while Masquerade is a game that forces you down one of two paths: you're either a psychopathic Machiavellian archetype following some Path of Enlightment, or you're actually tied down to your Humanity. Nevertheless, Humanity in Masquerade becomes just another tracker you have to keep relatively stable, like an oxygen meter in a video game or Humanity in Cyberpunk.

But that's not what being a creature of the night is. A vampire has a spectrum of emotions and paths they can follow. Vampires are humans forced into a world that doesn't know about them, where hierarchies far beyond their understanding exist, where they need to kill just to survive another day. This is obvious even from little details like how painful torpor is in Requiem, awakening mad and having had horrendous nightmares.

I really love Vampire: The Requiem. If you haven't tried it yet, give it a chance. I promise you won't be disappointed.

_G-man, late April '25_
